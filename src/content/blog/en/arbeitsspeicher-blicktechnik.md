## The Situation We All Know

A player plans her attack. She knows the block is set on the line, she knows the cross-court is wide open — and then she hits the ball with full power straight into the block's hands. Ask her afterwards and the answer is almost always the same: *"I didn't even see the block."*

She did see it. There just wasn't any room left for that piece of information at the moment of the hit. That is exactly what this post is about: **working memory** — and how to unload it so that the important things survive all the way to ball contact.

## What We Mean by "Working Memory"

When we talk about working memory, we mean ultra-short-term memory: everything we are currently directing our attention at. This memory has three inconvenient properties:

- **It is small.** Think of it as 4 to 8 slots.
- **It is individual.** Not everyone has the same number of slots available.
- **It is volatile.** Contents decay on their own after a short time. If we want to keep something longer, it almost takes an additional slot just to remind us that we are supposed to keep something.

We know this best from maths class. There are people who seem to solve everything in their head without writing anything down, while others constantly lose track halfway through. Attention makes the difference there too: someone who can hold three numbers, combine them straight away and remember the result looks gifted — when in fact that person mostly has more free slots for this particular domain.

In sport it works exactly the same way. At first glance volleyball looks incredibly complex. For "experts" who know **which** things need to be held in working memory, the game becomes surprisingly simple.

> The problem is rarely that we know too little. The problem is that we fill the slots with the wrong things.

## Example Reception: When the Plan Falls Out the Back

Let's look at reception. The ball is supposed to be delivered to a particular target. In the simple situation that's no problem:

I orient myself beforehand, the ball comes straight at me, I let it rebound in the prepared direction — good reception. But the ball often doesn't come straight to us. We have to move, and suddenly our position relative to the target no longer fits. Now everything has to be corrected with the arms:

All of a sudden we need a slot for ball contact, one for the target, one for the arm angle — and strictly speaking one for hips and shoulders too, that is, our position relative to the court. And the more stressful the situation gets, the more focus moves to slot 1: **just hitting the ball cleanly.** Everything else falls out the back.

That's the real problem: without a target and without orientation, even the cleanest ball contact gets us nowhere.

## Getting Better Means Needing Less Room

The better we get at something, the less room it takes up in working memory. There are two ways to get there:

1. **It doesn't have to enter memory at all.** The thing runs automatically.
2. **It needs less room.** Several related contents merge into a single slot — we call that **chunking**.

In reception, hip orientation, shoulder position and arm angle start out as three separate construction sites. Over time they become one single content: *"I am oriented towards my target."*

<MemorySlots scenario="annahme-vorbereitet" />

But this only happens if that information was ever in working memory in the first place. And because that is so exhausting, we do it far too rarely.

## Attacking: Why the Plan Never Makes It to the Hit

We know we should plan the attack: while the ball is still on the other side, we should already know where we want to place it and with which technique. Look at the result, though, and you usually see this: the placement is random, the technique is always the hard spike — often into the net or outside the court. That certainly wasn't the plan.

Why does this happen? Because we **jump to the ball**:

<MemorySlots scenario="angriff-zum-ball" />

Approach and take-off are continuously adjusted to the ball. This computation never stops and therefore eats up everything else. Once we are in the air and the ball is in front of the hitting shoulder, the only thing left in memory is: **"attack"** — that is, make contact. At what angle the ball crosses the net, or whether it crosses at all, no longer matters at that moment.

### The Thought Experiment

What if we didn't need all our attention for timing and position in the jump? My suggestion is simple: **instead of jumping to the ball, we jump into the attack zone** — and wait there for the ball.

From the quick attack through the middle we know that this works. On the outside positions it is harder because the ball travels further, but experience shows: the set is accurate enough for it.

<MemorySlots scenario="angriff-zone" />

It is still four contents — but two of them **dissolve during the action**:

- The approach timing is done once the approach is finished.
- The attack zone is done once we have taken off.

In the air we can't change our flight path anyway. So we don't need slots for it either, and we don't have to run complicated timing and position calculations. We simply wait for the ball. And precisely because of that, technique and target zone survive until the hit:

<MemoryPhases track="angriff" />

> The trick is always the same: **eliminate complexity before the action, so that there is still room for what matters at the decisive moment.**

## The Same Strategy for Reception

The complex part of reception is the time just before ball contact. So we shift the work forward: as soon as the serve comes, we **guess** where the ball is going and at what angle. Then we have half the flight distance to position ourselves so that we are already oriented towards our target. The shoulders also move into the "guessed" position ahead of time.

<MemoryPhases track="annahme" />

At the moment of contact we can release all those slots and only take care of the ball. Here too, then, we move into a predetermined, "empty" position so that everything afterwards becomes simpler.

## Serving: Clearing Out on Purpose

When serving we have plenty of time, because we control it ourselves. And yet we regularly make it unnecessarily hard for ourselves by deciding everything only at the moment of the hit:

<MemorySlots scenario="service-ungeplant" />

Of all things, the contact point and the hitting power — exactly what really counts at the moment of the hit — have no room left.

With a clean planning process we clear out the memory **deliberately**. And we do it backwards, against the ball's direction of flight:

<ServicePlanChain />

First we plan where the ball should land in the court. From there we estimate how high it has to cross the net. Maybe one intermediate step is needed. And only from that do we derive where and how hard we have to hit the ball.

The power we hit with is above all an internal feeling — we don't have to sacrifice a slot for it. What remains is:

<MemorySlots scenario="service-geplant" />

Two contents in working memory: we should be able to manage that. Only when the toss goes wrong do we need extra attention, because we have to re-establish our orientation to the court.

## Setting: Standards Instead of Calculations

Setting can be simplified almost completely as well. Really, the only thing that has to be in working memory is **where** the ball should go. The rest we handle beforehand:

- Guess as early as possible from which position we will play the ball.
- Go there and orient ourselves.
- The tempo, that is the height of the set, is standardised — we simply stick to it.

If the attacker isn't on time, that's not our problem. What remains are the target zone and the set height. And whoever gets better at that gets both into a single slot — and suddenly has capacity free for what really scores points: reading the opposing block.

## Gaze Technique as the Key

That gaze technique shows up here too is hardly surprising — it is almost always where we put our focus. Two directions are interesting:

1. How do we use working memory to **change** our gaze technique?
2. How does good gaze technique unload our **working memory**?

### Why the Gaze Sticks to the Ball So Stubbornly

Gaze technique is something highly individual and intrinsically learned. We can't simply switch it over, because our eyes are trained to look where they expect information. In volleyball that is **sometimes** the ball — namely exactly when we are the next one to touch it. In all other situations the ball holds no relevant information for us, because it will change direction at the next contact anyway.

That's what makes volleyball so hard: sometimes we have to look at the ball, but often we don't.

As beginners we learn to watch the ball very closely — that's the only way our own contact works. We then hold on to this pattern because it feels so proven. Those who become aware of it early make quick progress. Everyone else keeps reinforcing the bad gaze pattern, and it becomes very hard to break up again.

### Relearning Costs Slots — a Lot of Them

Fortunately, we humans can direct our focus deliberately, as long as we have capacity in working memory. If we want to relearn our gaze behaviour, we need several slots reminding us to guide the gaze differently. At the same time we still want to play the ball — and memory is volatile, so we need additional slots to refill the lost ones.

Let's look at what actually happens during a rally. The visualisation plays moment by moment — via "this run" you can compare the course so far at a glance:

<FocusRegeneration topic="blicktechnik" />

Memory gets filled from two sides:

- **From slot 1 downwards the situation pushes.** The opponent's body position, open zones in our own court, the flight path, ball contact — the contents change constantly, and they need different amounts of room. A flight path may sometimes get by with two slots and sometimes occupy four.
- **From slot 6 upwards the focus grows.** If there is room, it spreads out. Sometimes it grows well, sometimes not at all, sometimes it shrinks back by itself — without us having done anything wrong.
- **In between, everything is decided.** If the situation takes four slots, two are left for the focus. And at some point a big ball contact meets an already shrunken focus — and then it's gone completely.

That's exactly the point where the visualisation breaks off and starts over. That's not a presentation trick, it's the normal case: usually a newly set focus survives about one rally, sometimes not even three moments.

That is precisely why we need this element at all. We don't fill a slot with *"look at the block"* — steering the gaze directly doesn't work anyway. We fill a slot with the **focus on it**, and the gaze takes care of the rest by itself. The price: because every ball contact wipes it out, we have to set it anew several times per rally. Relearning isn't a one-off decision, it's **regeneration by the second**.

That also explains why it feels so exhausting — and why it works anyway. If we deliberately pack working memory with it, we succeed every now and then. And that's enough: once we have done it right a handful of times, we suddenly read so much information that our brain reinforces the pattern massively and very quickly. The effort feels extreme, but it doesn't last long — and the payoff afterwards is gigantic.

### The Same Principle for Everything Else

The focus slot is not a gaze-technique trick. It works for any content we are currently trying to relearn — for example for shoulder position and orientation in the court:

<FocusRegeneration topic="schulter" />

Here too we never occupy a slot with the movement itself, only with the attention on it. And here too: after every ball contact the slot is empty and has to be refilled.

### Losing and Resupplying

The decisive point: we can't prevent decay in working memory. But we can make sure that **every look delivers new content before the old one decays**.

<GazeMemoryCycle />

If the gaze sticks to the ball, every station delivers the same, quickly worthless information — a flight path that becomes invalid at the next contact anyway. Memory empties out. If instead the gaze travels to where information is being created right now, we do keep losing something, but we always have something usable in memory.

On top of that comes the biggest gain: the right gaze technique spares us the complex ball-trajectory computation that gets us nothing in most moments anyway. Those are slots we can spend directly on tactics.

## What This Means for Training

- **Decide before the action, not during it.** Anything settled beforehand costs no slot afterwards.
- **Think in zones instead of aiming at points.** Zones are coarse enough to fit into a single slot.
- **Plan backwards.** From the target to the contact point, not the other way round.
- **Standardise whatever can be standardised** (set height, approach rhythm, contact point).
- **Steer the gaze actively** — and accept that the transition makes everything else worse for a while.
- **One focus per drill.** Two coaching points at the same time are two occupied slots, and both disappear at ball contact.

## Conclusion

Everything we have thought through here fits beautifully with our previous theories — it even complements and reinforces them. The cycle is simple:

**Whoever unloads working memory makes everything else easier. And whoever makes everything else simpler needs less room in working memory — which again creates space for the things that decide a match.**
