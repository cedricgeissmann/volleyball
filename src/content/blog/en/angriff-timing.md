The spike in volleyball is one of the most demanding skills in any sport. Timing, athleticism, and technique all converge at once — and the time available is tight. But before we look at the numbers, let's establish the two fundamentally different ways you can attack in the first place.

## Form 1 and Form 2: Two Different Attacks

The goal sounds simple — hit the ball over the net. When the set is imprecise, a tempting solution emerges: just run under the ball, wait until it's almost on your head, and then jump. That's **Form 1** — reactive, ball-driven. The attacker waits, reacts, adapts. It works somehow, and that's exactly what makes it so hard to let go of.

The ideal attack looks like the opposite: the attacker jumps into the attack zone, and the ball comes to them. Not the other way around. That's **Form 2** — planned, setter-driven. The attacker runs their approach on their own timing, the setter delivers the ball into the zone, and both arrive at the same point in the air.

The critical difference: in Form 2, the attacker has to give up control completely. They need to be mid-approach while the setter still has the ball in their hands. They can no longer react to every imprecise set. This raises the error rate — at least initially. But these two forms are fundamentally different: playing Form 1 teaches you nothing about Form 2. Training Form 1 keeps you trapped in Form 1.

## How Much Time Is There Really?

Now that the difference is clear, the key question is: how much time is actually available for an attack — and what does that mean for timing?

We distinguish three tempos: Quick, Normal, and Out of System. What we call "Normal" is already a fast ball by many teams' standards — the simulation below shows why that makes sense.

It runs different sets and measures the time from the setter's contact until the ball passes through the attack zone. Setters aren't machines — every set carries small variations in the force applied. The simulation accounts for this and adds a random error on each run.

If you run the simulation several times, a pattern emerges: at normal tempo, the best compromise between limited time and greater precision becomes visible. The ball stays in the attack window for a comparatively long time — and yet there's barely more than a second left for the approach and jump. At quick tempo that window shrinks drastically; at out-of-system tempo it grows — but the long flight time makes the attack predictable.

## The Physics of the Set: Height Beats Distance

One surprising result from the simulation: the distance to the setter has no effect on the ball's flight time to the attack zone. That time depends exclusively on the maximum height of the set.

A set with more horizontal speed looks faster — especially to attackers standing far away. But that speed doesn't affect the time between setter contact and ball in the strike zone. What attackers perceive from a distance as threatening pace is an optical illusion.

This is why it's essential to read the angle of the ball — not its speed. Once timing can be read from the setter's body, this perception stops being an issue at all.

## From Form 1 to Form 2: What Needs to Change?

The simulation makes it measurable: for a Form-2 attack, the second approach step needs to already be complete when the setter contacts the ball. At that moment, the attacker doesn't yet know exactly where the ball is going. They have to trust the setter.

Small deviations can still be caught with arm reach or corrected with a slight adjustment in jump direction. That's enough — a set into the right zone is precise enough for an attacker to work with. The real problem is always timing: if it's significantly off, the attacker has no chance of doing anything with the ball in the air.

This is the paradox: a Form-1 attacker will always be too late for Form-2 attacks. The first step toward changing that is communication — and it runs in both directions.

**From attacker to setter:** The attacker communicates their timing directly through speed and distance from the net. They're running toward their attack position — the setter can read both location and timing in real time. This communication is unambiguous; it just needs to be read.

**From setter to attacker:** The setter always prepares for optimal contact in the same rhythm. A focused attacker can read exactly when that contact will happen from the setter's body position. All timing information can be taken from the setter's body — not from the ball in flight.

That's the shift: commit earlier, read the information at the right place, trust the setter. The change isn't easy — but when you know what you're looking for, it happens fast.

## Distance from the Net

A good Form-2 attack requires distance from the net — around 0.5 to 1 metre, depending on the situation. The takeoff happens well outside the 3-metre line, often 1.5 metres or more from the net.

This reveals another fundamental difference: Form 2 simply isn't possible if the attacker stays inside the 3-metre zone. The most important goal is therefore to get away from the net as early as possible — or better yet, to defend deep from the start.

Not every attack situation will be optimal — sometimes there isn't enough time or space for all four approach steps. Trying to remember when the first or second step happens won't help in many situations. The reliable reference point is the penultimate step: it happens in every situation regardless of step count, and is therefore the only timing anchor that always works.

## Conclusion

Form 1 works somehow — but it can't be converted into Form 2. Form 2 requires early commitment, trust in the setter, and the ability to read timing information in the right place: from the setter's body, not from the ball in flight.

The simulation makes the time windows concrete. Once you've seen that for a normal set the approach must already be well underway when the setter makes contact, it becomes clear why Form 1 is permanently too late — and what it takes to change that: not more strength or speed, but a different way of seeing the game.
