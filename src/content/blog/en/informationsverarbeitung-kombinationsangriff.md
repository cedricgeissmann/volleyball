## What is Environmental Information?

James J. Gibson devoted considerable thought to the question of how we perceive the world — and his answer was radical: **perception is direct.** We don't need to categorize, calculate, or interpret first. The environment offers us the relevant information directly, and our nervous system can respond to it immediately.

Gibson coined the term **affordances** for this — *offered possibilities*. An object or situation shows us directly what can be done with it, without us needing to know exactly what it is. A chair affords "sitting". It simultaneously affords "elevation". But not equally for every person: for a small child, that same chair does not afford sitting, because the child would first have to climb. For a tired person, the affordance is more present than for someone with energy. The affordance is always there — whether it is perceived is a different question.

A concrete physical example shows just how direct this perception really is: **Tau (τ)**.

---

### Tau — Direct Time-to-Contact Perception

Tau is an optical variable that the visual system can derive directly from the image — without explicitly measuring distance or velocity:

**τ = θ / (dθ/dt)**

θ is the current optical angle of an object (its apparent size in the visual field), dθ/dt is the expansion rate — how quickly that angle grows. The ratio directly yields the time to contact.

The key point: the visual system needs no conscious calculation. It responds directly to the expansion rate of the object.

Tau is the prime example of direct perception. We "see" the time to impact — we don't calculate it. The same principle applies to all relevant information in volleyball. The ball is often the *worst* source of information.

---

## The Ball Carries Too Little Information

This sounds counterintuitive at first. The ball is the center of the game, after all. But let's think it through:

The ball only reveals its current flight path. For most game situations, though, we don't need to know where the ball *is* — we need to know where it's *going* — and long before it gets there.

> **Core thesis:** All relevant information lies in the game situation — in the bodies, movements, and orientations of the players. The ball is only the end product of a situation that was already fully readable beforehand.

Let's look at this concretely.

---

## Sources of Information in the Game — Attack and Defense Reading Each Other

### Situation: The Ball is on the Other Side

The ball is on the opponent's side. I'm not allowed to touch it — a fundamental property of this situation. So what happens if I follow the ball anyway?

On the first contact, you see several players converging on the same target. That's where the ball will land. On the second contact, many players move away from the center of the court, while one moves with eyes to the ceiling toward the setting position — that's exactly where the ball arrives. On the third contact, the set, you can already see in the setter's hands which half of the court the ball will go to. Then you see which attacker is jumping — and more importantly: what timing they have. If the attacker doesn't have the timing, then I don't need to have it either.

> **Direct perception here:** The timing, direction, and quality of the attack can be read from the attacker's body — not from the ball. You can move directly with the player; with the ball, you always have to wait a moment to be sure where it's actually going.

---

### Situation: I Just Played the First Ball

As an outside attacker, I just received the ball. A fundamental property of this situation: I'm not allowed to touch it again — someone else has to do that for me.

This means: I can immediately move into my approach zone and observe the court only with peripheral vision. One player will clearly move to the ball — just before their contact I need to focus on them again. Their timing toward the set tells me when I need to start my approach. I don't need to watch the ball for that. The player slows down and shows a clear pattern of when they'll play the ball. All my timing information comes from that player — not from the ball.

Once I'm in the air, I look at the opponent's court and directly perceive the affordance "attackable" — or "tip-able" or "free ball". If an angle is open, I see it immediately.

> **Direct perception here:** Time in the air isn't "waiting for the ball". It's observation time for the opponent's court. The affordance "attackable" doesn't lie in the ball — it lies in the gap between the blockers.

## Combination Attacks — Simpler Than You Think

From the outside, combination attacks look like a coordination problem: two attackers have to synchronize their timing, position, and approach without knowing who will get the ball. That sounds like months of practice.

But it's only difficult if you're looking at the wrong information.

---

### Staircase Attack

In the staircase attack, the middle blocker and outside attacker approach right next to each other — the outside attacker runs approximately one step delayed and half a meter further outside.

> **Example situation:**
> Both read the setter slightly differently. The middle blocker needs to attack further left, the outside attacker further right. Both need to coordinate their timing without knowing who gets the ball. That sounds complicated and is indeed a nightmare in practice — if you look at it that way.
>
> Look at it differently, and the situation immediately becomes simpler: **The middle blocker goes for their attack. The outside attacker follows them with one step delay, offset half a meter to the right.** Until takeoff, the outside attacker only needs to watch their middle blocker. The coordination problem is completely solved — without any agreement, without a system, purely through direct perception of the teammate's movement.

<TreppeAttack />

Why does this work? Volleyball follows a simple physical principle: a body in motion maintains its motion. An attacker who has started their approach no longer changes direction or speed — unless the set doesn't come. The outside attacker therefore doesn't need to watch an uncertain ball, but a *very reliable movement*: that of the middle attacker.

> **Direct perception here:** The relevant information for the outside attacker lies neither in the ball nor with the setter — it lies in the movement of the middle attacker.

---

### Cross Attack

In the cross attack, two attackers run toward each other, cross paths, and jump at opposite positions. The block sees the crossing running paths and doesn't know who will get the ball.

<KreuzAttack />

> **Example situation:**
> The block tries to read the setter. It sees two players crossing — and at the moment of the crossing, there is briefly no reliable information about which attacker is going where. Whoever tries to read the ball in that moment loses valuable time. Whoever instead follows the players directly — their running direction, their body posture, their timing — immediately recognizes after the crossing who is jumping and where.

> **Direct perception here:** After the crossing, the situation is fully readable again. The player who is jumping shows it through their entire body posture. The ball delivers this information only with considerable delay.

---

### Stack Attack

In the stack attack, both attackers start one behind the other on the same line. The second attacker then breaks left or right at random — the defense doesn't know which direction until the last moment.

<StackAttack />

> **Example situation:**
> From a distance, the block sees two players running exactly the same line — until one breaks away. The moment of breaking happens very late and is the only difference from the previous phase. Whoever waits for the ball to decide reacts too late. Whoever observes the body rotation and first weight shift of the second attacker has the information earlier.

> **Direct perception here:** The break is not hidden information — it is visible in the attacker's body before the new running direction becomes obvious.

---

## Combination Defense — Whether We Want To or Not

Here is the crucial point: **In defense, we always form a combination.** We always have to coordinate with at least one other person. That's not a choice — that's the structure of the situation.

And defensive situations are the most complex in volleyball. No situation is exactly like the previous one. Whether a situation is "defendable" depends on: positions of all players, fatigue and speed of action, body orientation, blocking situation, angles of attack, and gaps on the court. That's a lot of information simultaneously.

This is precisely the moment where a fixed "defensive system" reaches its limits.

> **Example situation:**
> The ball is hit diagonally, just past the block, deep into the corner. The libero has to quickly straighten up and play the ball overhead with both hands. The attack comes again almost exactly the same way. The libero is smart and knows: this attacker plays long. They shift their center of gravity slightly backward to be able to come up faster.
>
> On the next attack, the block is a little higher, or the set a little lower, and the ball clips the block — a high tip toward the libero. Practically a free ball. But the libero has shifted their center of gravity backward and has no chance of getting forward in time. Panic breaks out with the setter and outside attacker, because they expect the libero to get the ball. But the libero just stands there, frozen.

Such situations arise when a defensive system replaces perception instead of supporting it. Responsibility is clearly assigned, but the situation has changed — and the system has no answer to that.

> **Direct perception here:** If P1 and P4 see that the libero has shifted their center of gravity backward, the changed situation is directly recognizable — they just have to react to it. Without this perception, communication is needed: P1 and P4 recognize that P5 won't reach the ball, have to come themselves, and still have to decide between themselves who plays it. This communication costs time — and in these situations, time is exactly what we don't have.

---

### Gaps in the Defense — The Dual of the Situation

How do we quickly find a gap in the defense? Affordance theory gives a clear answer: the affordance "ball on the floor" is always present — whether it is perceived is a different question.

> **Example situation:**
> Two defenders stand there, both thinking the other will take it. The ball drops between them. From the attacker's perspective, the gap was visible from the start — they aimed for it. From the defenders' perspective, the gap was not perceived, even though it was directly present in their situation.

That is the difference between the attacker and the defender in this situation: not reaction time, not athleticism — but **what information was perceived**.

The good news: if we as defenders recognize where "ball on the floor" *can* happen, we actually have the better hand. Because the longer the attacker waits, the fewer options they have. If we manage to observe all the relevant information — positions, orientations, the fatigue of our teammates — it becomes completely clear where an attack is going.

Unfortunately, this doesn't mean we can defend every ball. There are situations where an angle opens up at the last moment, or the attacker doesn't hit the ball cleanly, or the point is simply out of reach. But if we defend just **2 more balls per set** than the opponent — that can already be the difference between winning and losing.

> **Direct perception here:** The affordance "defendable" doesn't lie in the ball. It lies in the totality of the situation — in the positions, the bodies, the gaps. Whoever can read that directly reacts earlier, not faster.

---

## The Symmetry: Attack and Defense Read the Same Situation

This is the crucial insight:

**Combination attacks and combination defense are two sides of the same situation.** The attacker tries to use information about the defense. The defender tries to use information about the attack. Both simultaneously read the same situation — only from opposite perspectives.

This means:
- An attacker who reads well will still be stopped by a defender who reads well.
- An attacker who reads poorly will always be stopped by a defender who reads well.
- A defender who reads poorly will always be beaten by an attacker who reads well.

The ball is only the end product. **The information lies in the situation — and it is present for both sides simultaneously.**

## Implications for Training

Gibson's theory has an important practical consequence: the brain is not fully "wired" from the start. We have to build the connections that allow us to directly perceive a specific feature. That's exactly how it is with Tau — and exactly how it is with all other sources of information in volleyball.

This creates a problem: to improve our perception, we have to let go of our current perception. But we need our perception to orient ourselves in the game at all. In the short term, we will get worse. If we then try hard to get better again, the perception reverts to the old pattern.

The solution: we need to make the *new information source* directly accessible — so that the brain can no longer ignore this information. Not correcting the old perception, but offering a new one.

Concretely, this means:
- **Away from the ball** — even if it feels wrong. The ball is the worst source of information for everything except when you yourself have the next contact.
- **Watch the players** — their timing, their orientation, their movement quality. This information is always available earlier than the ball.
- **Actively name affordances** — not just passively observing a situation, but actively naming which affordance the situation offers.

---

## Conclusion

Combination attacks are not a coordination problem — they are a perception problem. And combination defense is the same problem from the other direction.

In both cases, the same holds: the ball carries too little information. All relevant information lies in the game situation — in the players' bodies, in their movements, in the gaps on the court.

Gibson was right: the environment offers us the information directly. We just have to learn to perceive it.
