## Introduction

Before we can examine this concept more closely, we need a shared foundation: what do we actually mean by **speed**?

For this article, speed is defined as **game speed** — the time that passes between the set and the attack. At high game speed, that window is as short as possible, as with a quick attack through the middle.

But the more important question is: **Why do we want high game speed in the first place?**

The answer is simple: to attack with as little block as possible.

## Speed Is Not the Only Tool

The quick attack is not the only way to attack without a block. The same goal can also be achieved by overloading an attack zone:

- With a combination of a net attacker and a back-row attacker
- As a combination attack between two net players

The objective: create a **time difference** between attackers that prevents the block from repositioning.

This same principle is what makes the outside attack combined with a quick attack such an effective combination. The ball is delivered to the outside attacker fast enough that the off-blocker cannot close in time.

The outside attacker is not block-free — but facing only one blocker, there is still plenty of space at the net. That makes it nearly impossible for the opposing defence to cover everything.

**And that is the goal:** to create the best possible situation for the attackers. That is game intelligence.

## The Core Rule: At Least Two Fast Attackers

That raises the question: **How do we make that happen?**

A simple answer: adjust the goal slightly. Instead of requiring *every* attacker to always be fast — which is not always possible — the rule becomes:

> **At least two attackers must always be fast.**

Why exactly two? Because only then is the block forced to make a decision, and time pressure can actually be created.

### Which Two Attackers?

That cannot be answered with a fixed rule. It is always **different** attackers — specifically the two who were not involved in the defensive phase.

They are free and can already transition into the next attack phase. That gets them into the attack zone sooner, which is what raises the game speed.

## Phases as Rate Limiters

The three-contact rule in volleyball divides the game into natural phases that every team and every player must move through.

### The Setter and Their Phases

A setter always has to work through the phases **Defence → Set → Block Cover**. The **Set** phase is skipped if the setter takes the first contact — but that is exactly the phase they cannot afford to skip, since it is their primary responsibility.

This is why complex rotations are designed to position the setter so they never have to pass. They can then move directly into the **Set** phase.

But that usually requires a run that must be completed very early. The setter needs to be at the net between positions 2 and 3 before the set happens. So the **Set** phase already demands a time-critical movement before the actual path to the ball even begins.

**The key:** that first movement must be completed as early as possible. Setters therefore often develop an instinct for moving directly into the next phase. With a free ball, the principle works the same way — the setter can skip the **Defence** phase and move straight into the **Set** phase, often before the ball has even crossed the net.

### Attack Readiness as the Critical Phase

But the setter is not the only one with phases. An attacker also moves through distinct phases:

**Defence → Attack Readiness → Attack**

Blocking counts as part of the **Defence** phase. If no attack follows, block cover applies — but the model is kept simple here because the focus is on the concept, not the details.

**Attack Readiness** is treated as its own phase because it is one of the most important and decisive points when playing fast and intelligently.

### A Concrete Example: The Quick Attack

Experience shows that the quick attack is highly effective at putting the opponent under time pressure. It often ends without a block — the point is simply won.

But the question worth asking: **Why don't we see this attack more often?**

It is not a question of defensive quality. The quick attack can be played effectively even from a position further from the net than the setter.

**The decisive factor is the attacker.**

They must be ready to swing in their attack zone at the moment the set is made. The ball travels very quickly from the set to the attack window. The simulations on attack timing in the [article on attack timing](/blog/en/angriff-timing) show that the ball's flight time is roughly equal to the time the attacker spends in the hitting window.

From this it follows: the attacker should already be jumping when the set contact is made. That gives them exactly the time the ball needs to travel from the defence to the setter — plus the approach step — for the **Attack Readiness** phase.

In other words: it is often difficult to achieve that in such a short time. Sometimes it is simply not possible.

### The Free Ball and Skipping a Phase

Consider the free ball: the middle blocker rarely plays it. They therefore get the opportunity to enter the **Attack Readiness** phase much earlier.

This only works, however, if the attacker actually makes that transition early. And this is exactly where game intelligence comes in:

> All that is needed is to recognise the situation. Then a phase can be skipped.

But that also requires knowing what the next phase is and what the role within it demands.

## Reading Situations: Observation and Intention

To do this, situations must be read — not by watching the ball, but by taking in the whole game and the movements on the other side of the net.

That requires:

1. **Observation** — to recognise which situation is unfolding
2. **Intention** — to know what the next phase is and what the task within it is

More on intention can be found in the article [Intention](/blog/en/intention) — Why your purpose makes all the difference.

This is left deliberately open here, because there are too many situations to list. Each player works through a phase slightly differently depending on their speed and strength. When something doesn't fit a phase, it becomes visible quickly and gets corrected by those around them.

## Pace on the Outside Positions

High pace is not only possible in the middle. It can also be created quite easily on positions 4, 2, and even 6.

The pace does not need to be as fast as a quick attack, but it should be fast enough to put the opponent under pressure. The same concepts apply — the challenge is often the time available for **Attack Readiness**.

When the **Defence** phase drops away, all of that time becomes available for **Attack Readiness** instead. That means:

- The approach can be started from a good position
- The attack target can be planned in advance
- There is even time to read the other side of the net and find where the defence is weakest

And the best part: when arriving early and high in the attack zone without the block closing in, the point can be found with a **well-placed** rather than a **powerful** attack.

## Speed vs. Stress

There is an important distinction between a **stressed** attack and a **fast** one. It is not always easy to tell the difference — in practice, attacks tend to be stressed rather than truly fast.

### The Decisive Difference: Preparation Time

With enough preparation time, an attack can be **relaxed and fast** at the same time.

Without preparation time, going fast means rushing to the ball — and arriving in an uncontrolled situation.

### The Extended Rule

The rule of at least two fast attackers can therefore be extended to:

> **At least two fast, relaxed attackers.**

### How to Measure It

How well this is working can be assessed subjectively with two simple questions:

- How much stress is felt on our side of the net?
- How much stress can be put on the opponent?

---

**Summary:** Modern volleyball lives from the balance between speed and intelligence. Speed alone is not enough — what makes the difference is applying it correctly, timing it right, and reading the situation accurately.
