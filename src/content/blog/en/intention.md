Intention is one of the most important things we can bring to volleyball. Without it, we don't control the ball — we just react to it.

## Two intentions, two completely different movements

When a ball comes toward us, we face a decision we rarely make consciously: What do I want to do with this ball? Keep it off the floor — or play it to the setter?

That sounds like the same goal. It isn't. A player who only wants to save the ball runs straight into its path and does the easiest thing: hits the ball in the direction they're already running. The result? The ball lands somewhere — usually far from the setter.

A player who intends to pass to the setter, on the other hand, aligns their shoulders toward that target — before the ball is even touched. The decisive difference lies in **shoulder alignment**. It determines where the ball goes after contact.

The simulation shows exactly this moment: player B runs toward a randomly placed ball and makes contact. Without intention, the shoulders follow the direction of movement — the natural result when the only goal is to reach the ball at all. The ball flies wherever B happens to be running: somewhere, but rarely to A.

With intention, something different happens. Because B knows from the start where the ball needs to go, the shoulder axis aligns perpendicular to the target line — regardless of which direction B is running from. That requires a deliberate body rotation while still moving. That is the core of intention: not reacting to the ball, but playing it with control.

The same applies to attacking. When the set isn't perfectly in line, the attacker approaches at a slight angle. If the only goal is to "hit the ball hard," they contact it from the wrong alignment — and the ball flies off-target, often wide or into the net. The defence barely needs to move.

## Intention trains attention

The right intention also changes what we look at — and that matters at least as much as technique.

A player who just wants to "hit hard" doesn't need to know where the block is or whether the net is in the way. But a player who intends to land the ball on the opponent's side of the court automatically starts tracking block position. Intention directs attention.

This is especially clear with the tip. Young players almost always tip short, just behind the net — because it works in training where there's no real block. In a match, three opponents are paying close attention. A short, low tip straight over the net is almost always a bad idea.

The right intention when tipping: **play the ball where the opponent has to travel the furthest.** That means reading the block, seeing the gaps, identifying open court. That awareness never develops if the intention is simply "get the ball over the net somehow." Players who don't read what's happening on the other side never learn to read it — and can't build a game against the opponent.

## The right intention in training: out-of-system, not outright point

In a match, we want points. In training, that's the wrong intention — at least when attacking.

A player who tries to score on every attack in training aims for empty space. The ball hits the floor, the defence never touches it, and nobody learns anything. That feels like success but it's wasted training volume — for both sides.

The more useful intention in training: **place the attack so it's difficult to defend, but still playable.** Don't force the point; force an out-of-system situation. That means putting the defence under pressure while still giving them a chance to make contact.

What changes: the attacker now has to genuinely read the defence — where are they standing, which area is hardest for them to cover? This trains exactly the kind of attention that matters in a match. At the same time, the defence has work to do: they get difficult balls, practise playing under pressure, and learn to build an offence from a poor position.

A player who always attacks into empty space learns neither to read nor to place. And the defence learns nothing at all.

## What this means for learning

Intention doesn't only determine what happens now — it determines what we learn over time. Players who always just react, learn to react. Players who play with a clear intention learn what that intention points them toward.

There's a second factor: to learn something new technically, the body needs enough **degrees of freedom** — room to experiment and adjust. A practical rule of thumb: stay below 80% of maximum effort. Not to go easy on yourself, but because above that threshold the nervous system falls back on established movement patterns and closes off anything new.

Imagine sprinting as fast as you possibly can. Your legs have no choice — they just have to run. The faster you go, the less you can correct or adapt. The same happens when attacking at full power: the body can't learn because there's no room left to explore.

In practice: a player who always swings as hard as possible in training only reinforces the strength of an already familiar pattern — not precision, adaptability, or new technical solutions. Drop the intensity slightly and that room opens back up.

## What this means for training

Before you play a ball: decide consciously where it should go. Not "somewhere toward the setter," but a specific position. That decision changes everything — the shoulders align, attention follows, and the ball goes where it's supposed to.

With the wrong intention — saving the ball instead of playing it with control — you learn to save balls. That's useful, but it's not volleyball. With the right intention, you learn to read the game, shape it, and find technical solutions you would never have found without that intention.

Intention isn't just a mental trick. It's the starting point for everything else.
