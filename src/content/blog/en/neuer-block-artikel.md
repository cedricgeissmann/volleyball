Blocking is considered one of the hardest skills in volleyball. It does not have to be. Once we understand what blocking is actually about and what we need to pay attention to, progress comes surprisingly fast.

Block height is the one thing we can barely influence — that takes body size and jumping power. This article is not about that. We work on the three things anyone can improve right away: **concept**, **timing**, and **technique**.

## The Concept: Blocking Is Defense

There is hardly a nicer point than a kill block. But it is not the idea of the block. Strictly speaking, every kill block is a setting or hitting error by the opponent. A blocker cannot influence the play in a way that *forces* a kill block to happen.

But if we are not looking for the direct point with our block — why do we jump at all?

To understand that, we have to drop the ego perspective and think as a team. The goal of the block is to **block** the attack: either keep the ball on the other side, or at least take away a large part of the hitting area. The block is the first line of defense. Only because of it does a defense become organizable at all — without a block there are simply too many angles to cover.

From that follows the most important sentence in this article:

> Our job is never to score the point. Our job is to limit the opponent.

If a point comes out of it, we will happily take it. But it is never the goal.

## The Block Window

The key question at the net is: **where do we place the block?** Almost as important is the timing — we will get to that right after. First we need to understand what the block window is and how we find it.

## Gaze Control: The Right Information at the Right Time

The hardest part of blocking is not the jump, it is where you look. There are many sources of information, and we have to switch between them within fractions of a second.

1. **The setter**: here we see whether we have to block at all, or whether we drop off to defend.
2. **The ball**: if the set comes our way, we have to determine very quickly and very precisely where the ball is going. After that, the ball gives us no new information.
3. **The attacker**: from now on everything sits with him — timing, angle, and type of hit. That is what allows us to fine-tune the block.

Exactly this fast switch from the set to the attacker, including working out timing and angle, is the main difficulty in blocking.

## Timing

The timing comes more or less directly from the attacker: **if we jump with the attacker, we are on time.** Later does not work.

Most blocking errors have their cause right here — we are late, or we cannot hold our position in the air.

## Closing the Block

Closing the block sounds simple, but in practice it often fails. The usual instruction is: *the outside blocker sets the block, the inside blocker only closes to it.* If you follow that, the inside blocker only has to watch his partner's arms and bring his own hands alongside.

It becomes a problem when the outside blocker drops away. There are two scenarios to distinguish here.

### Scenario 1: The Outside Blocker Closes the Line

This is not a problem — or should not be. The logic is simple: if the outside blocker sees the attacker hesitate and look for the line, then everyone else sees it too. All of them can adjust at the same time. The result is a blocked ball, or enough angles taken away to defend it.

### Scenario 2: The Block Falls Away Involuntarily

This is where it gets dangerous. The other players do not see the reason for the opening and therefore cannot adjust. The block opens up, the defense panics, and the hardest angle suddenly becomes available to the attacker. It hardly gets worse than that.

The blame then tends to land on the middle blocker for not closing the block. Usually it is not his fault — he simply could not see it coming. The actual cause sits with the outside blocker: he loses stability in the air, or carried too much momentum from the start.

The block window solves exactly this problem. If you know before the hit where you want to block, you can also keep that window closed. And if both blockers identify the same window and want to keep it blocked, coordinating a double block suddenly becomes easy.

## Coordination: Windows as Asynchronous Communication

We already know windows from attacking and setting. They are our most important tool for solving timing and positioning problems — because in volleyball we cannot plan everything sequentially. We need a form of **asynchronous communication**:

- The **setter** needs the setting window and a fixed height.
- The **attacker** needs the attack window and stable timing.
- The **blockers** need the block window.

That way everyone can focus on their own part, and clean coordination still emerges.

At the block it is even more demanding: here we are looking for a shared solution with someone who is actively trying to prevent it. All the more reason why the block window is central for us.

## Summary

- The block does not score the point — it limits the opponent and makes the defense organizable.
- The **block window** tells us where to block. It is the shared reference point for all blockers.
- The information switches in this order: setter → ball → attacker.
- We take the **timing** from the attacker: jump with him, never later.
- An involuntarily opened block is the most expensive error — stability in the air and a clearly identified window prevent it.
